  • NOT FINISHED maximum range of major tenth, check other sources to see if this applies to all melodies

  • NOT FINISHED Perfect consonances: unison, fifth, octave; intervals made of these and the octave. Imperfect consonances: sixth, third; intervals made of these and the octave. The fourth is debated: some consider it a perfect consonance, others a dissonance (do any consider it an imperfect consonance? Do all the sources that follow Fux also have his "sometimes consonant, sometimes dissonant"?). All others are dissonant.

    Unison, third, fifth, sixth, octave, and the intervals made up of these and the octave are consonances. Some of these are perfect consonances, the others imperfect. The unison, fifth, and octave are perfect. The sixth and third are imperfect. The remaining intervals, like the second, fourth, diminished fifth, tritone, seventh, and the intervals made up of these and the octave, are dissonances.[4.1]

    In an earlier chapter, Fux distinguishes between the fourth obtained from the arithmetical division of the octave and that deriving from the harmonic division... In the first case, where the lower tone of the fourth is at the same time the fundamental tone—that is, in every instance when dealing with two voices—the fourth is considered a dissonance. In the second case its dissonant character is invalidated by the new fundamental tone, and it can be considered an imperfect consonance... In classifying the fourth among the dissonances, Fux makes his decision with regard to what he calls "a famous and difficult question." Martini, basing his opinion upon that of Zarlino (Institutioni Harmoniche, Part III, ch. 5), goes so far as to call the fourth a perfect consonance (Esemplare, pp. xv and 172). Hadyn and Beethoven follow Fux. Mozart (Fundamente des General-Basses, p. 4) also lists the fourth as a dissonance.[4.2]

    The consonant intervals are the P1, m3, M3, P5, m6, M6, and P8. All other intervals within the octave are considered dissonant.[5.1]

    From a perfect consonance (P1, P5, P8) to an imperfect consonance (M3, m3, M6, m6, M10, m10)...[5.3]

  • should be 8–16 notes long

    Preference for moderation in length (8-15 notes)[1]

    length of about 8–16 notes[2]

    as a rule the cantus will not contain fewer than eight or more than sixteen tones.[6.2]

    Two sources said 8–16, one said 15, so 8–16 won. The program allows you to choose the length of your cantus firmus; if you prefer 15 you can simply refrain from choosing 16, so I did not bother to code a config that changes the bounds on length from 8–16 to 8–15. I may reverse that decision at some point.

  • all notes are the same duration, and are best written as whole notes

    All notes are of equal duration (by convention, the whole note)[1]

    arhythmic (all whole notes; no long or short notes)[2]

    [First species] is the simplest composition of two or more voices which, having notes of equal length, consists only of consonances. The duration of the notes is unimportant except that it should be the same for them all. Since the whole note, however, gives the clearest picture, I think we shall employ it in our exercises. With God's help, then, let us begin composition for two voices. We take as a basis for this a given melody or cantus firmus...[4.3]

    ... the cantus firmus must consist of tones of equal time value… It is best, therefore, to write the cantus in whole notes; smaller values would almost inevitably suggest metrical organization, the periodic alternation of strong and weak pulses.[6.1]

  • all notes are diatonic, with the exception of some specific cases with modal cantus firmi

    Only diatonic tones are used (major system only at present)[1]

    Completely diatonic. No chromaticism.[3]

    Although I only have citations for two sources, all example cantus firmi I have seen so far (exists in 5/6 sources) are purely diatonic, with some very specific and predictable exceptions related to modal cantus firmi—these will be discussed later. The first source's note "(major system only at present)" also reinforces my choice to accommodate for non-diatonic tones only in the case of modal cantus firmi. That source is the notes of a professor teaching a class. There would be no need to single out the "major system only" if that rule was not meant to be broken at some point later on when modal cantus firmi were introduced to students.

  • maximum range of a major tenth

    Maximum range of a 10th[1]

    range (interval between lowest and highest notes) of no more than a tenth, usually less than an octave[2]

    Range no more than a tenth.[3]

    The total range of a melody should rarely exceed an octave and should never exceed a tenth.[5.2]

    A tenth between the lowest and the highest tone is the maximum range.[6.2]

    No source has specified whether the tenth is major or minor, which is exceedingly frustrating. I have gone with the major tenth as the absolute limit, assuming that if there are any cases in which it is actually a minor tenth, chromaticism requirements will enforce the restriction for me, leaving any major tenth outright illegal to move to for reasons other than a maximum range restriction. There is likely a reason they wouldn't clarify "major tenth" or "minor tenth", likely because it can and will change with context.

  • first and last notes are both the tonic, and in same octave

    First and last notes are both tonic, and in same register[1]

    begin and end on do[2]

    Begin and end on tonic.[3]

    If the cantus firmus is to form a self-contained whole... it must begin and end on the tonic.[6.4]

    Although only one citation specifies "same register", I assume this is because of most cantus firmi being under an octave (as stated in citations for the "maximum range of a major tenth" rule)—this means only one tonic is in range, so the question of which octave's tonic to end on is usually unnecessary (but worth specifying as ranges may be large enough to reach two different octaves' tonics!).
