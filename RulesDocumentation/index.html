Rules

General

  • Most cantus firmus rules (exceptions exist) apply to other species.

    In [first species'] horizontal construction, the added voice is governed by the rules and tendencies set down for the c.f. There are two exceptions: the added voice is permitted to repeat a tone, and it may begin and end in different registers.[1.2]

    The [second species of] counterpoint aims for melodic fluency and equilibrium just as the c.f. does. It is therefore governed by the same rules and tendencies...[1.3]

    What was said about the linear requirements for the added voice in the second species applies to the added voice in the third species as well.[1.4]

    The [fourth species of] counterpoint again strives for melodic fluency and equilibrium, and is therefore subject to the same rules and tendencies as the c.f.[1.5]

    The following set of rules invoked in all the species begins with the melodic matters discussed already [regarding the cantus firmus. This sentence is followed by a list of rules for melodic writing in general, many of which appear in other sources' rules for the cantus firmus.][2.1]

    In general, the counterpoint should follow the principles of writing a good cantus firmus discussed in the previous chapter, Introduction to Species Counterpoint. There are some minor differences, to be discussed below...[2.2]

    Learning to compose and recognize good cantus firmi is essential because their characteristics are present in all good melodic lines... You will compose your counterpoint melodies following most of the same rules you learned for cantus firmus writing. There will be a single climax, motion mostly by step, leaps must be treated properly, etc.[3]

    [This source has a section titled "Melodic Characteristics", beginning with "Melodies in species counterpoint (both the cantus firmus and the contrapuntal melody)", implying the rules outlined in this section apply to all melodies: both a cantus firmus and various species of counterpoint.][5.2]

    The principles governing the writing of cantus firmi are fundamental to te study of counterpoint; in all five species the same basic considerations of melodic organization prevail.[6.1]

    The melodic character of the added line or [first species] counterpoint resembles that of the cantus firmus. In writing a two-part [first species] counterpoint, therefore, we shall make use of the same general principles of melodic construction that we learned in the section on cantus firmus.[6.5]

    For the most part, the principles of melodic organization studied in the chapters on cantus firmus and first species prevail in second species as well.[6.11]

    For the most part, third species follows the same melodic procedures as second.[6.12]

    Cantus Firmus

  • should be 8–16 notes long

    Preference for moderation in length (8-15 notes)[1.1]

    length of about 8–16 notes[2.1]

    as a rule the cantus will not contain fewer than eight or more than sixteen tones.[6.2]

    Two sources said 8–16, one said 15, so 8–16 won. The program allows you to choose the length of your cantus firmus; if you prefer 15 you can simply refrain from choosing 16, so I did not bother to code a config that changes the bounds on length from 8–16 to 8–15. I may reverse that decision at some point.

  • all notes are the same duration, and are best written as whole notes

    All notes are of equal duration (by convention, the whole note)[1.1]

    arhythmic (all whole notes; no long or short notes)[2.1]

    [First species] is the simplest composition of two or more voices which, having notes of equal length, consists only of consonances. The duration of the notes is unimportant except that it should be the same for them all. Since the whole note, however, gives the clearest picture, I think we shall employ it in our exercises. With God's help, then, let us begin composition for two voices. We take as a basis for this a given melody or cantus firmus...[4.3]

    ... the cantus firmus must consist of tones of equal time value… It is best, therefore, to write the cantus in whole notes; smaller values would almost inevitably suggest metrical organization, the periodic alternation of strong and weak pulses.[6.1]

  • all notes are diatonic, with the exception of some specific cases with modal cantus firmi

    Only diatonic tones are used[1.1]

    leading notes in all cases, including in “minor”-type modes for which that seventh degree needs to be raised[2.1]

    Completely diatonic. No chromaticism.[3]

    Although I only have citations for two sources, all example cantus firmi I have seen so far (exists in 5/6 sources) are purely diatonic, with some very specific and predictable exceptions related to modal cantus firmi—these will be discussed later. The first source's note "(major system only at present)" also reinforces my choice to accommodate for non-diatonic tones only in the case of modal cantus firmi. That source is the notes of a professor teaching a class. There would be no need to single out the "major system only" if that rule was not meant to be broken at some point later on when modal cantus firmi were introduced to students.

  • maximum range of M10

    Maximum range of a 10th[1.1]

    range (interval between lowest and highest notes) of no more than a tenth, usually less than an octave[2.1]

    Range no more than a tenth.[3]

    The total range of a melody should rarely exceed an octave and should never exceed a tenth.[5.3]

    A tenth between the lowest and the highest tone is the maximum range.[6.2]

    No source has specified whether the 10th is major or minor, which is exceedingly frustrating. I have gone with M10 as the absolute limit, assuming that if there are any cases in which it is actually a m10, chromaticism requirements will enforce the restriction for me, leaving any M10 outright illegal to move to for reasons other than a maximum range restriction. I suspect this is the reason they didn't specify M10 or m10, likely because it can and will change with context.

  • first and last notes are both the tonic, and in same octave

    First and last notes are both tonic, and in same register[1.1]

    [Regarding first species, in] its horizontal construction, the added voice is governed by the rules and tendencies set down for the c.f. There are... exceptions:... it may begin and end in different registers.[1.2]

    begin and end on do[2.1]

    Begin and end on tonic.[3]

    If the cantus firmus is to form a self-contained whole... it must begin and end on the tonic.[6.4]

    Although only one source specifies "same register", I assume this is because of most cantus firmi being under an octave (as stated in citations for the "maximum range of M10" rule)—this means only one tonic is in range, so the question of which octave's tonic to end on is usually unnecessary (but worth specifying as ranges may be large enough to reach two different octaves' tonics!). And that one source that specifies "same register" also goes on to say that an "exception" to a c.f. rule is first species being allowed to end in different registers; further reinforcing the "end in same register" rule for a cantus firmus.

  • "extreme" tone (either highest or lowest present in cantus firmus) that appears once in cantus firmus

    Apex (highest tone)... must occur only once[1.1]

    a single climax (high point) that usually appears only once in the melody[2.1]

    Must have a single and unique climax, either a zenith or a nadir.[3]

    Each cantus firmus must contain a climax or high point… The climax tone should not be repeated. It is possible to construct a line in which the climax is the lowest rather than the highest tone. Such lines are generally most effective in a bass register.[6.3]

    Two sources acknowledge the possibility of a nadir, all of a zenith.

  • intervals between sequential notes must be melodic consonances: m2, M2, m3, M3, P4, P5, m6, M6, P8

    No dissonant leaps are allowed (the P4 is consonant in melody only)[1.1]

    all note-to-note progressions are melodic consonances[2.1]

    Mostly, but not entirely, by step... No dissonant melodic intervals... Melodic consonances are major and minor seconds[, m]ajor and minor thirds and sixths[, p]erfect fourths, fifths, and octaves... Never write a dissonant melodic interval (a diminished fifth or an augmented fourth, or a major or minor seventh)[3]

    Specifically the cantus firmus should not contain... dissonant leaps... The dissonant leaps include all sevenths and all augmented and diminished disjunct intervals... The usable intervals, therefore, are the following: major and minor seconds, major and minor thirds, perfect fourths, perfect fifths, major and minor sixths, and perfect octaves.[6.2]

    The third citation says it most clearly. The fourth implies that the "usable intervals" are consonant by way of contrasting them with the aforementioned "dissonant leaps".

    First Species

  • one note for each note of the cantus firmus

    In the first species, exactly one note of counterpoint is set against each note of the c.f.[1.2]

    In first-species counterpoint, we begin with a cantus firmus (new or existing) and compose a single new line—called the counterpoint—above or below it. That new line contains one note for every note in the cantus: both the cantus firmus and the counterpoint will be all whole notes.[2.2]

    In first species, there is a one to one relation between the CF and the CTP. In other words, for every one note of the CF, there is a CTP note of equal value.[3]

    To each of these [cantus firmus] notes, now, should be set a suitable consonance in a voice above...[4.3]

    In first species counterpoint you will write one note in the counterpoint for each note in the cantus firmus (c.f.).[5.3]

    In first species, the added voice, like the cantus firmus, moves in whole notes; the contrapuntal texture, therefore, is note-against-note.[6.5]

  • maximum range of M10

    It... should... cover an overall range of no more than a tenth.[3]

    Note the general rule saying most cantus firmus rules apply to other species. Then look at the cantus firmus rule setting the maximum range to M10.

  • M10 is max interval between cantus firmus and first species counterpoint

    The interval between the cantus firmus and counterpoint at any moment should not exceed a perfect twelfth (octave plus fifth). In general, try to keep the two lines within an octave where possible, and only exceed a tenth in “emergencies” and only briefly (one or two notes).[2.2]

    Don't exceed a tenth between voices.[3]

    In first species counterpoint, the only intervals allowed between the two voices are M3, m3, P5, M6, m6, P8, M10, and m10.[5.5]

    Given the last citation went out of its way to include compound interval of the 10th (octave plus third) when most don't, I assume it would have included the next harmonic compound interval (12th, octave plus fifth), if it were included. It didn't, and the first citation that claims a limit of a 12th itself reserves going above a 10th for "emergencies". So I resolve in favor of a 10th.

  • the only legal vertical relationships between the cantus firmus and first species counterpoint are harmonic intervals: P1, M3, m3, P5, M6, m6, P8, M10, and m10.

    The only intervals that may be used vertically are consonant intervals. [1.2]

    Harmonically, the intervals between the two voices are all consonances.[2.2]

    Use only consonant harmonic intervals: perfect fifths (P5), perfect unisons and octaves (PU and P8), major and minor thirds (M3 and m3), and major and minor sixths (M6 and m6).[3]

    [First species of counterpoint] is the simplest composition of two or more voices which, having notes of equal length, consists only of consonances.[4.3]

    In first species counterpoint, the only intervals allowed between the two voices are M3, m3, P5, M6, m6, P8, M10, and m10. The unison is not acceptable in any place other than the first and final measure of the exercise.[5.5]

    First-species counterpoint uses only consonant intervals. These are: perfect unisons, major and minor thirds, perfect fifths, major and minor sixths, and perfect octaves.[6.6]

    I assume M10 and m10 were left out of most sources because they are compound intervals, and their inclusion is implied.

  • a counterpoint written above the cantus firmus must start in unison with, or a perfect fifth or octave above, the cantus firmus' first note; a counterpoint written below the cantus firmus must start in unison with, or an octave below, the cantus firmus' first note

    The upper counterpoint may begin with either the unison, the 5th, or the 8ve of the c.f. tone. The lower counterpoint may begin only at the unison or 8ve.[1.2]

    When writing a counterpoint above a cantus firmus, the first note of the counterpoint should be... a P1, P5, or P8 above the cantus... When writing a counterpoint below a cantus firmus, the first note of the counterpoint must always be... P1 or P8 below the cantus firmus... beginning on... P5 [would] confuse listeners about the tonal context... [2.2]

    counterpoints... can start on 1̂ or 5̂ when above the cantus firmus... Counterpoints must begin on 1̂ when below the CF.[3]

    Figure 2.1: Permissible opening intervals for two-voice counterpoints [shown in the figure to be an 8, 5, or U when the cantus firmus is below the counterpoint; a U or 8 when the cantus firmus is above the counterpoint][3]

    The beginning and the end... both must consist of perfect consonances.[4.4]

    [A perfect fifth below the cantus firmus is marked as incorrect with the justification that "the counterpoint must be in the same mode as the cantus firmus" and this "obviously force[s] the beginning out of the mode."][4.6]

    If the counterpoint is above the cantus firmus, the first note of the counterpoint should be a P1, a P5, or a P8. If the counterpoint is below the cantus firmus, the first note of the counterpoint should be a P1 or a P8.[5.4]

    The unison, therefore, will occur only at the beginning and end of the first-species exercise.[6.8]

    In the upper counterpoint the first tone may be an octave or fifth above the cantus... it is usually difficult to begin the upper counterpoint with a unison...[6.9]

    The lower counterpoint will begin with a unison or octave. The fifth below the tonic will throw the exercise out of the key; therefore it may not occur.[6.10]

    Keep in mind for source 3 that because cantus firmi start on the tonic, on scale degree 1, first species starting on scale degree 1 would include both unison and octave, which is backed up by the figure cited from source 3.
    Though source 6 says "In the upper counterpoint the first tone may be an octave or fifth above the cantus",[6.9] excluding the unison, it also says "The unison, therefore, will occur only at the beginning and end of the first-species exercise"[6.8]. The citation excluding the unison also includes a mention of how it is difficult to begin the upper counterpoint with a unison. Given all the other sources, the mention of unison occurring at the beginning of first species,[6.8] and guidance from Professor Ibbett, I take the exclusion of the unison in [6.9] not to be this source saying that the unison is disallowed for the beginning of an upper counterpoint in first species, merely that it is difficult to do so.
    All other sources are straightforward about this rule.

  • the final note of a first species counterpoint must be P1 or P8 apart (both down and up acceptable) from the final note of the cantus firmus

    The added voice must close on the tonic tone, at the interval of either a unison or an 8ve with the c.f.[1.2]

    The final note of the counterpoint must always be... P1 or P8 above/below the cantus[2.2]

    Counterpoints... end with the scale degree progression 7̂—1̂...[3]

    End on a PU or a P8 as part of clausula vera[3]

    The final interval, therefore, will be an octave or unison.[6.10]

  • the penultimate note of a first species counterpoint must be 7̂ if the penultimate note of the cantus firmus is 2̂; the penultimate note of a first species counterpoint must be 2̂ if the penultimate note of the cantus firmus is 7̂.

    In the penultimate bar, if the c.f. tone is scale degree 2, the added voice must have scale degree 7, and vice versa.[1.2]

    always approach the final interval by contrary stepwise motion, as follows:
    • If the cantus ends 2̂—1̂, the counterpoint’s final two pitches should be 7̂—1̂.
    • If the cantus ends 7̂—1̂, the counterpoint’s final two pitches should be 2̂—1̂.
    Thus, the penultimate bar will either be a third or a sixth between the two lines... This ending formula is known as the clausula vera.[2.2]

    Counterpoints... end with the scale degree progression 7̂—1̂...[3]

    ...in the next to last bar [when they have been using whole notes for each note, so the next-to-last note] there must be a major sixth if the cantus firmus is in the lower part; and a minor third, if it is in the upper part.[4.4]

    Thus, the seventh degree has to be raised in the Dorian (D), Mixolydian (G), and Aeolian (A) modes. (The second degree of a mode occurs always as the next to the last tone in the cantus firmus, the seventh degree always as the next to last tone in the counterpoint.)[4.5]

    The final interval, therefore, will be an octave or unison.[6.10]

  • the interval between the cantus firmus and the first-species counterpoint may not be P1 except at the first and last intervals

    The unison must not occur in the main body of the exercise; its use is restricted to the first or the last bar (see 1 and 7).[1.2]

    In general, all harmonic consonances are allowed. However, unisons should only be used for the first and last intervals.[2.2]

    The PU is used only to begin or end a phrase in first species, never within the phrase.[3]

    ...the unison should nowhere be employed in this [first] species of counterpoint, except at the beginning and the end.[4.7]

    In first species counterpoint... the unison is not acceptable in any place other than the first and final measure of the exercise.[5.5]

    The unison, therefore, will occur only at the beginning and end of the first-species exercise.[6.8]

  • no similar or parallel movement to a P5 or P8 apart between the cantus firmus and first species counterpoint

    Perfect consonances may be approached only by contrary motion or oblique motion, never by similar motion (whether parallel or nonparallel)[1.2]

    Never use two perfect consonances of the same size in a row: P5—P5 or P8—P8.[2.2]

    Never move into a perfect consonance by similar motion (this is called direct fifths/octaves.[2.2]

    Perfect intervals approached only by contrary motion never parallel or similar motion[3]

    From one perfect consonance (P1, P5, P8) to another perfect consonance, proceed in contrary or oblique motion.[5.5]

    From an imperfect consonance to a perfect consonance, proceed in contrary or oblique motion.[5.6]

    Parallel motion in fifths, octaves, and unisons must be avoided.[6.7]

    ...we shall avoid the approach by similar motion to the perfect intervals.[6.7]

  • no voice crossing

    Avoid voice crossing[: w]hen a higher voice part moves below a lower voice part. In strict SATB style, the ranges of voices should not cross; the soprano must always be higher than the alto, the alto must always be higher than the tenor, and the tenor must be higher than the bass.[2.2]

    No voice crossing.[3]

    The crossing of parts, i.e., temporarily shifting the lower voice above the upper or the upper voice below the lower... it is best at this stage to exclude crossings...[6.9]

  • no voice overlapping

    Avoid voice overlap, where one voice leaps past the previous note of the other voice. For example, if the upper part sings an E4, the lower part cannot sing an F4 in the following bar.[2.2]

    No overlapping of voices.[3]

    If, without crossing parts, the lower voice moves to a tone higher than the preceding tone of the upper voice, or if the upper voice descends to a tone lower than the previous one of the lower voice, the parts are said to overlap... avoid the use of the overlap.[6.9]

  • no more than 3 parallel imperfect consonances in a row

    Do not use more than three of the same imperfect consonance type in a row (e.g., three thirds in a row).[2.2]

    No more than three parallel thirds or parallel sixths in succession.[3]

    Avoid extended passages in parallel motion. For the most part, there should be no more than three successive parallel thirds or sixths.[5.6]

    ...we must somewhat restrict the use of parallel thirds, sixths, and tenths. In general, no more than three such intervals should appear in succession.[6.7]
